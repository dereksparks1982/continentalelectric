General Electric: the historical roots

General Electric was formed in 1892 when J. P. Morgan organized the merger of Thomas Edison’s Edison General Electric Company and Elihu Thomson’s Thomson-Houston Electric Company. Thomson-Houston executive Charles A. Coffin became GE’s first president.

Edison’s manufacturing operations had moved to Schenectady, New York, in 1887. GE’s formation there in 1892 helped make Schenectady one of the central places in the history of the American electrical industry.

The lineage matters because it was not the story of one inventor or one product. Edison, Elihu Thomson, Edwin Houston, Coffin, financiers, engineers and factory workers all fed into a corporation that could move from lighting and electrical equipment into power generation, transportation, communications, appliances, research and major industrial projects.

What inspired Federal Electric

Federal Electric borrows the shape of that industrial story, not GE’s identity. The game begins with a small 1938 light-bulb manufacturer and leaves room to grow into components, radios and electronics, utility equipment, industrial machinery, government contracts, major infrastructure and eventually wartime production.

General Electric, Westinghouse and Sylvania are historical reference points for the kind of American electrical-industry growth the simulation is exploring. Federal Electric remains its own fictional company, with its own decisions, successes, scandals, products and alternate history.

One scale example is Hoover Dam. The Bureau of Reclamation records that the first two of the initial four 82,500-kilowatt generators were awarded to General Electric, while the other two went to Westinghouse and a 40,000-kilowatt unit went to Allis-Chalmers. That kind of leap from ordinary electrical manufacturing into enormous public infrastructure is the sort of long-term opportunity Federal Electric may eventually pursue.
